**Motivation:**
The statement suggests that increased intracellular lysine levels in Saccharomyces cerevisiae are associated with decreased resistance to acetic acid. This experiment aims to explore whether manipulating intracellular lysine levels affects the yeast's resistance to acetic acid, potentially unveiling a link between lysine metabolism and stress response mechanisms.

**Hypothesis:**
Increasing intracellular lysine levels in Saccharomyces cerevisiae reduces the yeast's resistance to acetic acid, potentially due to lysine's influence on cellular stress response pathways or membrane integrity.

**Experimental Plan:**

1. **Strain and Media:**
   - Use a reference/wild-type prototrophic S. cerevisiae strain.
   - Grow cells in minimal media without amino acids, except where lysine supplementation is specified.

2. **Supplementation Levels:**
   - Control: No lysine supplementation.
   - Low lysine: Supplement with 1 mM lysine.
   - High lysine: Supplement with 10 mM lysine (supraphysiological concentration).

3. **Acetic Acid Treatment Levels:**
   - Control: No acetic acid.
   - Treatment: 20 mM acetic acid (a concentration known to impose stress on yeast cells).

4. **Negative Control:**
   - Use leucine as a negative control to ensure that the observed effects are specific to lysine. Supplement with 10 mM leucine in a parallel set of wells.

5. **Experimental Conditions:**
   - Each combination of lysine supplementation and acetic acid treatment will be tested:
     - No lysine, no acetic acid
     - No lysine, 20 mM acetic acid
     - 1 mM lysine, no acetic acid
     - 1 mM lysine, 20 mM acetic acid
     - 10 mM lysine, no acetic acid
     - 10 mM lysine, 20 mM acetic acid
   - Include leucine controls with and without acetic acid:
     - 10 mM leucine, no acetic acid
     - 10 mM leucine, 20 mM acetic acid

6. **Measurements:**
   - Measure OD600 every 20 minutes to monitor growth and viability.
   - At experiment termination (20 hours), use mass spectrometry-based metabolomics to measure intracellular lysine levels.

7. **Assumptions and Controls:**
   - Verify that lysine supplementation effectively increases intracellular lysine levels through metabolomics.
   - Include appropriate controls to ensure that observed effects are not due to other factors, such as osmotic stress from amino acid supplementation.

**Expected Outcomes:**
- If increased intracellular lysine levels reduce resistance to acetic acid, we expect to see a more significant growth inhibition in the presence of 20 mM acetic acid in the high lysine condition compared to control and low lysine conditions.
- If lysine does not affect resistance, growth inhibition should be similar across all lysine supplementation levels when exposed to acetic acid.
- Leucine controls should not exhibit decreased resistance to acetic acid, confirming lysine's specific role.
- The results will help clarify the relationship between lysine metabolism and chemical stress resistance in S. cerevisiae.

### Motivation

The statement suggests an association between elevated intracellular lysine levels and decreased resistance to acetic acid in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Understanding this relationship can provide insights into how amino acid metabolism influences stress responses, particularly to organic acids, which are common stress factors in industrial fermentation processes.

### Hypothesis

Elevated intracellular lysine levels negatively impact the resistance of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to acetic acid, potentially due to lysine's role in metabolic pathways that modulate stress responses or cell wall integrity.

### Experimental Plan

**Objective:** To test whether increased intracellular lysine levels are associated with decreased resistance to acetic acid in Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

**Experimental Setup:**

1. **Strain:** Use a reference/wild-type prototrophic strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

2. **Media:** Minimal media without amino acids, except for specified supplementations.

3. **Supplementations:**
   - **Lysine Levels:**
     - Control: 0 mM lysine
     - Low: 0.5 mM lysine
     - High: 5 mM lysine (supraphysiological concentration)

4. **Acetic Acid Treatment:**
   - Control: No acetic acid
   - Treatment: 30 mM acetic acid

5. **Negative Control:**
   - Use threonine at 5 mM (equimolar to the highest lysine concentration) as a negative control to ensure that any observed effect is specific to lysine and not a general amino acid supplementation effect.

6. **Experimental Conditions:**
   - Temperature: 30°C
   - Shaking: 200 rpm
   - Duration: 20 hours

7. **Measurements:**
   - Intracellular lysine levels: Measured at experiment termination using mass spectrometry-based metabolomics.
   - Growth curves: OD600 measured every 20 minutes to assess cell viability and growth.
   - Resistance assessment: Compare growth curves in the presence and absence of acetic acid.

**Experimental Combinations:**
- 3 lysine levels (control, low, high) x 2 acetic acid conditions (control, treatment) = 6 conditions
- Negative control with threonine (treated and untreated) = 2 conditions
- Total of 8 conditions

### Expected Outcomes

1. **Lysine Supplementation:**
   - Increased intracellular lysine levels should be observed in the lysine-supplemented conditions compared to the control.

2. **Resistance to Acetic Acid:**
   - If the hypothesis is correct, cells with higher intracellular lysine levels (5 mM supplementation) will show decreased resistance to acetic acid, evidenced by reduced growth or viability in the presence of acetic acid compared to the control and low lysine conditions.
   - The negative control with threonine should not show a significant change in resistance, confirming the specificity of the lysine effect.

3. **Independent Effects:**
   - Any growth differences in the absence of acetic acid across lysine levels will indicate the independent effects of lysine on cell physiology, unrelated to acetic acid resistance.

By systematically varying lysine levels and acetic acid presence, this experiment will elucidate the relationship between lysine accumulation and chemical resistance in yeast.
